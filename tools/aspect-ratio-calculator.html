How it works

The tool divides width and height by their greatest common divisor (GCD) to produce a simple ratio such as 16:9. To scale, it multiplies the known dimension by the ratio: if the width is known, height = width × (ratio height ÷ ratio width).

When to use this calculator

  • Checking whether a photo will fit a print size without stretching or unwanted cropping.
  • Resizing an image for the web while keeping the original proportions.
  • Comparing a camera or screen ratio with standard formats such as 4 × 6 prints or 16:9 video.
  • Working out the missing dimension when you know one side and the target ratio.

Worked examples

  • 1920 × 1080 simplifies to 16:9, the standard widescreen and video ratio.
  • 6000 × 4000 simplifies to 3:2, the classic 35 mm and 4 × 6 print ratio.
  • 1200 px wide at 3:2 gives a height of 800 px - a common web image size.
  • 3000 × 2400 simplifies to 5:4, which matches 8 × 10 prints.

Ratio is not resolution

The ratio describes the shape; resolution describes how many pixels make up that shape. A 1920 × 1080 video frame and a 3840 × 2160 monitor are both 16:9, but one has four times the pixels of the other. Use the megapixel calculator to compare pixel counts and the print size calculator to see how large each will print.

Common mistakes

  • Resizing one dimension only, which stretches faces and circles into ovals.
  • Confusing a ratio with a size: "16:9" says nothing about how many pixels or inches.
  • Assuming every print size shares the same ratio - 8 × 10 (5:4) and 4 × 6 (3:2) do not.
  • Rounding a ratio too early and accumulating errors across a resize.

Frequently asked questions

What does a 16:9 aspect ratio mean?

It means the width is 16 units for every 9 units of height, such as 1920 × 1080 or 3840 × 2160.

Will scaling change the quality of my image?

Scaling the canvas or cropping changes composition, not the source file. Upscaling pixels beyond the original resolution cannot add true detail and may look soft.
