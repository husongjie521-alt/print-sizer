What Is 300 DPI?

300 DPI means 300 dots per inch: a printer places 300 dots of ink in every linear inch, which equals 90,000 dots per square inch. For digital images, the equivalent term is 300 PPI (pixels per inch), and the two are treated as the same thing when preparing files for print.

Do you always need 300 DPI?

300 DPI is the standard for documents, brochures and photo prints viewed up close. Large posters and canvas prints are usually viewed from farther away, so 100–200 DPI is normally enough. A 24 × 36 in poster at 300 DPI would need a 7200 × 10800 px image; at 150 DPI it only needs 3600 × 5400 px, which is achievable with a 24 MP camera.

If your print service asks for 300 DPI, follow their instruction. If they give a pixel requirement instead, compare it with your image dimensions - the pixel requirement is the number that actually matters.

How to check whether your image is 300 DPI

  1. Open the image properties (or an editor such as Photoshop or GIMP).
  2. Look for resolution metadata labelled DPI or PPI; many cameras record 72 or 96 for screen, but the metadata does not change image quality.
  3. Calculate the real question: how many pixels does the image have, and what physical size do you need?

Frequently asked questions

If an image says 72 DPI, can it still print well?

Yes. The DPI tag is just metadata. What matters is the pixel count and the print size. A 6000 × 4000 px image tagged 72 DPI still prints at 20 × 13.3 in at 300 DPI.

What is the largest print size at 300 DPI for 24 megapixels?

A 6000 × 4000 px image prints at 20 × 13.3 in at 300 DPI. You can print larger by lowering DPI or using print services that accept lower resolutions for larger products.

How 300 DPI affects file size

Doubling the resolution of an image quadruples its pixel count. A 10 × 8 inch image at 150 DPI contains 1500 × 1200 px; at 300 DPI the same physical size requires 3000 × 2400 px - four times the data. That is why 300 DPI files are larger and why upscaling a small file to reach 300 DPI does not create new detail.

300 DPI on phones and scans

A modern phone photo is typically 12–48 MP, which is more than enough for most 300 DPI prints up to A3 or slightly beyond. Scans are different: scanning a photo at 300 DPI captures 300 pixels per inch of the original print, which is usually sufficient, while scanning film negatives benefits from 1200–2400 DPI because the original frame is so small.

Rule of thumb

  • Hand-held print: 300 DPI.
  • Canvas: 200 DPI.
  • Poster: 150 DPI.
  • Backdrop or billboard: 30–100 DPI depending on distance.

When in doubt, ask the print service for pixel dimensions rather than a DPI figure - pixels are unambiguous.
